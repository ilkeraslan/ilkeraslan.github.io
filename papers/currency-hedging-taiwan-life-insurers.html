Accounting Rules, Currency Hedging and External Adjustment: Evidence from Taiwan’s Life Insurers

Ilker Aslan · Lecturer, Kalaidos University of Applied Sciences, Zürich

Download PDFAll papers

Abstract

Taiwan’s current-account surplus reached 19.6 per cent of GDP in 2025, and its life insurers are the main channel through which it is invested abroad. Whether they hedge determines how much of the surplus reaches the spot market and who bears the currency risk. I study an accounting change, effective in January 2026, that lets insurers amortise exchange differences on foreign bonds held at amortised cost over the bonds’ remaining lives. In a model in which insurers penalise the variance of both economic and reported earnings, the response of the hedge ratio identifies the share of hedging done to protect reported earnings. The sector’s hedge ratio fell from 59 to 43 per cent between November 2025 and June 2026, about NT$2.4 trillion of hedges or two-fifths of the 2025 surplus; the largest insurer cut its ratio by about 19 points in the month the draft appeared. At least 30 per cent of prior hedging served reported earnings. The cost of offshore forward hedges fell below that of onshore swaps, and the Taiwan dollar weakened by 0.5 to 0.7 per cent against regional currencies on the two most informative announcements. Futures prices show the dollar’s forward discount against the Taiwan dollar closing in the six weeks after the change was proposed. Interbank turnover in outright forwards fell by more than a third; swap turnover did not change. Accounting rules for institutional investors are a channel of external adjustment.
