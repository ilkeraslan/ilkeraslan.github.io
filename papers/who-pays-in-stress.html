Who Pays in Stress? Ownership and the Funding Costs of Life Insurers

Ilker Aslan · Lecturer, Kalaidos University of Applied Sciences, Zürich

Download PDFAll papers

Abstract

Supervisors worry that life insurers owned by alternative asset managers are fragile in credit stress, but most of these insurers are unlisted and regulatory data are slow. I measure how creditors price them in the secondary market for funding agreement-backed notes, a wholesale liability issued by insurers of every ownership type, using 861 notes of 24 insurers from 2016 to 2026. Notes of insurers owned by alternative managers yield 22 basis points more than notes of the same maturity and rating traded on the same day, a difference carried by the largest issuer. The premium is contingent on credit conditions: it rises by 20 basis points for each 100 basis point increase in the high-yield spread, an effect that neither the rating nor the volume of notes outstanding explains and that remains at 12 basis points without the largest issuer. Three of the six such insurers, including the two largest, are the three most stress-sensitive issuers in the market; the other three resemble conventional insurers. Statutory filings show that the three hold the highest shares of affiliated bonds, but neither these holdings nor illiquid assets explain the premium. The same premium appears in the senior notes of the groups' holding companies. The premium widened in March 2020 and March 2023 but not during the private credit concerns of 2025. Share prices of insurers that cede liabilities to alternative managers carry no comparable signal. Creditors differentiate by ownership in the states that matter for stability, raising refinancing costs when assets are hardest to sell.
